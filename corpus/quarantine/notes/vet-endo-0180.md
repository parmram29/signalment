## Dietary imbalances and pathophysiology

Nutritional secondary hyperparathyroidism develops when dietary Ca:P ratios are inverted (high phosphorus, low calcium) or when vitamin D is deficient. Common causes include excessive grain feeding, inadequate roughage, or inappropriate supplementation. Growing animals and lactating females are at highest risk.

## Bone remodeling consequences

Chronic PTH elevation causes excessive osteoclastic activity, resulting in fibrous osteodystrophy. Bones become soft and prone to spontaneous fractures. The skull may develop characteristic thickening (big head disease). Dental abnormalities include malformed teeth and delayed eruption.

## Prevention and management

Proper Ca:P ratios (typically 1.5-2:1) with adequate vitamin D prevent the condition. Treatment involves correcting dietary imbalances and providing calcium supplementation. Recovery depends on the extent of skeletal changes and duration of the condition before intervention.
